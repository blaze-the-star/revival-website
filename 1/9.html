Revival Ch.9

Joseph woke to stillness.

Not the ordinary stillness of late night.
This was different—a quiet that felt held.
Like the world had stopped breathing.

He lay curled in his tent, blanket pulled to his chin.
The canvas above him held faint silver moonlight.
Nothing moved.

But something was wrong.

No sound. No rustle.
Just a pressure. A presence.
The sense he was not alone.

Joseph held still.
Forced his breathing even.
His ears strained, searching.

Then—

A whisper of movement.
Fabric against grass.

Someone was inside the tent.

His body went rigid.
Mind raced—scream, run, fight—but his limbs wouldn't move.

A paw touched his shoulder.

He flinched—scrambled backward, blanket tangling, a yelp catching—

“Hush.”

Low. Urgent. Female. But not steady.
A paw pressed against his chest, almost shaking.

“Be still. I'm not here to hurt you.”

Joseph's eyes adjusted.
Slim shape.
Upright.
Ears and muzzle barely visible.
The scent reached him: earth, moss, something sharp and green—mixing so much he couldn't tell if he recognized her or not.

“Who—”

“Quiet.”
She breathed it.
“You want them to find me?”

His heart slammed.
He stared, trying to find threat or safety in the darkness.

She leaned closer.
Voice barely a whisper.

“My name's… Pearl.”
It came out strained—like the word hurt.
“I need to talk to you. We can't do it here.”

Pearl.
The name flickered—a shape without edges.

“I don't—”

“I know you don't know me,” she said soft, urgent.
“But I know you.”
“The shrine walk. The wind. The God you wouldn't bow for.”

Joseph's breath caught.

“I've been looking for you. Not to hurt you. Just to—” she hesitated.
“Just to understand.”

Tent flap rustled.
Voices from camp—low, indistinct.
Fight on watch.

Pearl went still.
Coiled.
Ready to vanish.

The voices faded.

She exhaled.
Turned back.

“I can get you out without them hearing. But you have to trust me.”
A pause.
“Can you do that?”

Joseph stared.

Every instinct said call out.
Wake Snout.
Let Eli handle this stranger who moved like smoke.

But something held him.

Not trust.
Not yet.

Curiosity.

Because she'd said understand.
Because she'd come in the dead of night, risked everything, just to talk.
Because God crossed her lips like a question she couldn't put down.

Slowly, he freed himself from the blanket.

“…Okay.”

Pearl moved before he finished—smooth, silent.
Eased the flap open, reached back.

Joseph crawled out after her.

Night air hit cold and sharp.
Camp lay quiet.
Embers glowed.
Fight's silhouette stood at the clearing's edge, back turned.

Pearl pressed close.
Thread of sound.

“Stay low. Move when I move. Don't look back.”

She crept forward.
Joseph followed.

Every step too loud.
Every breath a betrayal.
Heart hammering—surely Fight would hear.

But there was no shout.
No alarm.

Then the treeline.

Pearl pulled him into darkness between trunks.
They kept moving—faster now, putting distance between themselves and the glow.
Only when Joseph couldn't have found his way back did she slow.

She stopped in a moonlit clearing and turned.
Joseph could see her properly now.

A fox.

The moon caught along the ridge of her back and the sharp line of her cheek, but the rest of her dissolved into dark.
She was tall compared to him—
but so was every other fox he'd seen.

Joseph stood there, coat clutched around himself, trembling from cold and adrenaline and the sheer strangeness of it all.

Pearl watched him.

They didn't speak at first.

She led through the trees—purposeful, practiced.
Joseph followed, too shaken to ask where, too tired to do anything but keep moving.

Cold bit through his coat.
No blanket.
Ears ached.
Legs trembled.

Pearl glanced back, noted his shivering, said nothing.

They emerged onto the road to Flint Water.
The town lay ahead, dark and quiet under stars.
River murmured.
A sign creaked.

Joseph stopped in his tracks.

Flint Water.   The place Eli had barred him from.
Where priests raised staves and crowds knelt.

Pearl turned.

“What?”

“I'm not supposed to be here alone.”

“I know.”

Simple. Acknowledging.
She walked back, close enough that he caught that sharp green scent.

“Eli's trying to protect you. I get that.”
“But I'm not gonna hurt you.”
“And I'm not gonna let anyone else hurt you either.”
“Not tonight.”

Joseph stared.

“Why should I believe that?”

She met his gaze.

“You shouldn't. Not yet.”
Head tilt.
“But you're here anyway.”
“So either you're desperate, or some part of you trusts me already.”

Joseph opened his mouth—found he couldn't argue.

Because she was right.
Some part of him had trusted her.
Enough to follow.
Enough to leave the only safety he'd found.

He didn't know what that said about him.

Then something rose beneath it—cool and clear.

Wait.

He looked at her again.
Really looked.

The way she'd appeared in his tent.
The way she'd moved—silent, practiced.
The way she'd spoken: so either you're desperate, or some part of you trusts me already.

It was a good line.
A convincing line.

A Manipulative line.

His eyes narrowed, and he took a small step back.

Pearl's brow furrowed.

“What—”

Another step.

“What are you doing?”

He spoke—voice level, quiet, steady.

“Trying to figure out if I made a mistake.”

Pearl's ears flicked back.

“I told you, I'm not going to hurt you.”

“You told me a lot of things.”
Another step. Distance widening.
“But you haven't shown me anything.”
“Except that you can get into tents without waking anyone.”

Her jaw tightened.

“I masked my scent. So I wouldn't spook the others.”
“That's not—”

“That's planning. Skill.”
“You knew exactly what you were doing.”

Another step back.

Her tail went still.

“What do you want me to say? That I'm dangerous?”
“Fine. I'm dangerous. I've been dangerous for years.”
“That doesn't mean I'm lying about why I came.”

“Then show me.”

Joseph stopped.
Small and shivering, coat clutched tight—but something in his posture had shifted.
A stillness that wasn't fear.

“Convince me. Not with reason. Tell me something real.”
“Something you don't want me to know.”

Pearl stared.
Silence stretched.
An owl called.

Joseph waited. Present. Patient.
Like he had all the time in the world.
Like he wasn't the one being led.

Pearl's expression flickered—through things he couldn't name—before settling on something raw that made his chest ache.

“You're supposed to follow. You're supposed to just—”
She broke off, shaking her head.
“That's how this works. I lead. You trust. That's the deal.”

Joseph shook his head slowly.

“I didn't agree to any deal.”

Her voice dropped.

“Then why'd you come?”

Genuine now.
Uncertain.
Almost fragile.

Joseph met her eyes.

“I've been asking myself that same question.”

Another step back.

“And if you can't give me a reason to stay—something real—then I'm leaving.”

Her ears flattened against her skull.

“You can't. You don't even know the way back.”

“Then I'll wander until I find it.”
Calm. No heat. Just truth.
“I'd rather be lost in the dark than follow someone who's only showing me what they want me to see.”

Another step.

Pearl lifted her paw—not reaching, just moving, like she didn't know what to do with her body.

“That's not—”
She stopped. Swallowed.
“You don't understand.”

“Then help me understand.”

Another step back.

Her breath came sharper now. Quicker.
The mask cracking—eyes darting, tail curled tight.

“I can't just—I don't do this. I don't stand in roads and—”

“Then walk away,” his voice gentle.
“Go back to Flint Water. Forget you found me.”
“I won't tell anyone.”

He meant it.

And somehow, she knew.

Pearl stood frozen.
Caught between town and road, between the story she'd prepared and this small, stubborn little fox who wouldn't be led.

“Tell me.”
The words soft.
“Not what you want me to know. Not what you think will work.”
“Tell me the truth about why you came.”

Another step back.

The distance real now.
Cold seeping through his coat.
Dark pressing around him.
If she didn't speak—if she let him walk—he was ready.

Pearl scoffed.

“This is stupid. I should never have come out here.”

Joseph's next move was simple.
He nodded, and turned around.
Then—he started walking.

One step.
Two steps.
Three steps.

Pearl's eyes flickered disbelief.
She turned her head away, and swallowed a faint growl.

Her voice broke.

“I'm tired.”

Joseph stopped.
He heard it in her voice.
For the first time that night, she wasn't a threat, a manipulator, or a practiced survivor.

She was tired.

Just tired.

“I'm tired of being Soot,” she whispered.
“I'm tired of being the one everyone watches. The one everyone expects to fail,” her voice cracked.

“I’m tired of fighting everyone.”
A swallow.
“I just did something right once—just once.”
“And it burned everything I loved.”

Joseph half turned back to her.

Soot.

The name washed over him all at once.
Snout's voice echoed in his memory—troublemaker…
thief…
tried to steal from Eli once.

The warnings.
The suspicion.
The way Snout's whole body had tensed at the mention of her.

This was her.

This was the one they'd told him to be wary of.

He looked at her—really looked.
At the wet eyes.
The trembling tail.
The way she held herself like someone braced for another blow.

She noticed his stillness.
Something flickered in her expression—fear, maybe.
Or resignation. Like she'd finally said too much and now the judgment would come.

“Soot,”
Joseph repeated quietly.

She flinched.

“That's—”
She started to pull back, to rebuild the walls.
“I know what they say about me. If you want to leave, I—”

“I'll come with you.”

Soot blinked—like she hadn't expected that.
Like some part of her had braced for rejection.

“Why?”
Rough. Almost defensive.
“I just told you—I'm Soot. I'm the one everyone—”

“I heard you,” his voice gentle.
“You told me your name. Not who you are.”

Soot stared at him.

For a moment, she looked utterly lost—like no one had ever said anything like that before.

Then she turned sharply, facing the road.

“Come on. It's cold, and I know a place with a fire.”

She started walking.

Joseph followed—walking beside her.

Soot led him through the sleeping streets of Flint Water with the confidence of someone who knew every shadow, every alley, every door that opened to the right touch.

They passed the market where Joseph was nearly struck.
He looked away, but not fast enough—the image burned behind his eyes: the staff raised, the crowd kneeling, the wind—

“Don't think about it,”
Soot said quietly, not looking back.
“Not now.”

Joseph swallowed and kept walking.

They stopped at a narrow building wedged between a cooper's shop and a darkened stall.
No sign marked its purpose.
No light showed through its shuttered windows.
Soot produced a key from somewhere in her coat and unlocked the door, gesturing for Joseph to enter.

He stepped inside.

The room was small but warm—a single space with a low ceiling, a cold hearth, shelves crowded with jars and bundles, and a pallet in the corner covered in blankets.
Herbs hung from the rafters, filling the air with a complex, earthy fragrance.
A table sat in the center, scattered with papers and the remains of a meal.

Soot closed the door behind them and bolted it.

“Home,” she said dryly.
“Such as it is.”

Joseph stood in the middle of the room, uncertain where to put himself.
His legs ached.
His paws were numb.
The warmth was starting to reach him, and with it came a wave of exhaustion that made his vision swim.

Soot noticed.

“Sit down before you fall down,” she said, not unkindly.

She moved to the hearth, bent low to arrange kindling and strike flint.
Sparks caught.
A small flame flickered to life, then grew, casting orange light across the room.

Joseph sank onto the edge of the pallet, too tired to ask if he was allowed.
He pulled his coat tighter, watching Soot work.

She fed the fire methodically, adding small sticks, then larger ones, until the warmth began to spread in earnest.
Only when she was satisfied that the little flames would take did she rise and turn to face him.

For a long moment, neither spoke.

Then Soot settled onto a stool near the hearth, legs crossed, tail curling around her feet.
The firelight caught the edges of her fur, the sharp lines of her face—but the sharpness wasn't there anymore.
Not the way it had been.

She looked at him, and for once, she didn't seem to be calculating.

“I don't know what to say now,” she admitted quietly.

Joseph blinked.

Soot shook her head slowly, staring into the flames.

“On the road, I—”
She stopped. Started again.
“I didn't plan to tell you any of that. The name. The—”
Her jaw worked.
“Any of it.”

Joseph nodded carefully.

“I know.”

She looked at him.

“How?”

“Because you fought it.”
His voice was gentle.
“Every step back I took, you fought giving me something real. Until you couldn't anymore.”

Soot held his gaze for a long moment.
Then she looked away.

“Yeah,” she breathed.
“That's—”
A rough exhale that might have been a laugh.
“Yeah.”

The fire crackled.

Joseph waited.

Soot's voice came quieter now.

“I’ve kept my walls up so long I forgot what it feels like to put ’em down.”

Her tail curled around her paws.
“And now they're down and I just feel—” She shook her head.
“Empty. Like there's nothing underneath.”

Joseph shifted on the pallet, wincing as his stiff muscles protested.

“That's not true,” he said.

Soot's ears flicked.

“You don't know that.”

“I know you came for me.”
He met her eyes.
“I know you stood on a road and told me something you didn't want to tell anyone.”
“I know you're sitting here right now, not running, not deflecting, not—”
He gestured vaguely.
“Being whoever you have to be out there.”

Soot stared at him.

“Why are you still here?”
The question came out raw, unguarded.
“I got what I wanted. You followed. We're inside. I could've just—”
She gestured vaguely.
“Started asking questions. Pushing. Doing what I do.”

Joseph considered that.

“Why didn't you?”

Soot opened her mouth.
Closed it.

“I don't know,” she admitted.
“I just—” she looked away.
“Didn't want to. Not with you.”

The words hung between them.

Joseph felt something shift in his chest—not heavy, not light, just…
Present.

“Thank you,” he said quietly.

Soot's brow furrowed.

“For what?”

“For not.”
He shrugged slightly.
“For just… sitting here. For letting me sit here too.”

Soot was quiet for a long moment.

Then, barely audible:

“I meant what I said. On the road.”
She stared into the fire.
“I am tired. I've been tired for years. And I don't—”
She shook her head.
“I don't know what to do with that. I don't know what comes next.”

Joseph nodded slowly.

“Me neither,” he said.
“Most days.”

Soot looked at him sharply.

“You? You've got—”
She gestured vaguely.
“All of it. The God stuff. The peace stuff.”
“The standing in squares and not dying stuff.”

Joseph huffed a quiet laugh.

“That's not—”
He shook his head.
“That's not me. That's Him. I'm just…”
He trailed off, searching for words.
“I'm just someone who got pulled into something bigger than myself and hasn't figured out how to stop being scared of it.”

Soot studied him.

“You don't act scared.”

“I act scared all the time.”
Joseph's ears flattened.
“I just do it quietly”

Something flickered in Soot's expression—recognition, maybe.
Or kinship.

The fire popped. Its light had grown while they talked.
For the first time, Joseph could see her clearly—
the sharp lines of her face, the exhaustion beneath, the black fur, and her eyes.

Blue.

He hadn't known they were blue.

Soot's voice came softer now.

“Tell me about Him.”

Joseph looked at her.

“Not because I want an argument,” she added quickly.
“Not because I'm trying to—”
She stopped. Took a breath.
“I just… I need to know if it's real. If He's real. If someone like me could ever—”

She stopped again.

Joseph waited.

Soot's shoulders dropped.

“When you stood in that square,” she said quietly,
“when you said my God doesn't let me bow—I felt something. In my chest. Like—”
She pressed a paw to her sternum.
“Like something knocked. And I've been trying to ignore it ever since.”

She looked at him directly.

“So tell me. Please. Not because I'm ready. Not because I believe. Just because I need to know what I felt.”

Joseph breathed.

Then, slowly, he began to speak.
He sure how.
He didn't know what to say.
All he had was what he knew, so he shared it.

He told her about God.
The One he'd known since before he could remember.
The One who'd been with him in a forest when he woke up scared and alone.
The One who'd stayed.

Soot listened.

As he spoke, she shifted—sliding off the stool, settling onto the floor with her back against it.
Joseph eased down from the pallet, finding a place on the worn boards across from her.
They sat facing each other now, fore-paws on the ground, supported by their own weight, the firelight flickering low between them.

She didn't interrupt.
Didn't scoff.
Didn't offer opinions or corrections or advice.

She just sat there in the firelight, wrapped in her own fur and her own silence, and let him talk.

He told her about his Savior.

A God who loved so much that He became small.
Who walked among the people He'd made.
Who touched the unclean and ate with the broken and let a woman wash His feet with her tears.

“A God who gets dirty,” Soot murmured.

Joseph nodded.

“A God who gets dirty so that we could be clean.”

He told her about the cross.

About a man who was willingly nailed to wood, bleeding out while the crowd watched, while the ones who loved Him scattered and hid.

“Why?” Soot asked quietly.

Joseph met her eyes.

“That's—”
She shook her head.
“That's stupid. Why would He—” she stopped.
“Why would anyone do that?” she whispered.
“For people who don't deserve it?”

Joseph's chest ached at the question—at what it revealed about her.

“Because,” he said gently.
“He sees who you can become, who you were always created to be.”

The fire crackled.
Soot was quiet for a long time.
Then:

“How do you know? That He's real? That any of it's real?”

Joseph was quiet for a moment.

“For a long time I didn't,” he admitted.
“But one day He showed himself to me, and He cared about me.”
He met her eyes.
“Not just the important things—He cared about what mattered to me.”
His voice softened.
“And I asked how… How could You?…”

Soot leaned in.

“He said that—”
Joseph swallowed.
“He said 'I feel nothing but love for you.' ”

Soot didn’t speak.
The fire shifted between them.

“That’s stupid,” she whispered, yet not angry.

Time moved strangely after that.

The fire had burned lower, the room softer.
At some point they'd both shifted—Joseph stretched on the floor near the hearth, Soot lying across from him, her head on her paws.
The flames cast long shadows across the ceiling.

The questions came in hungry waves.
Soot asked about prayer.
About sacrifices.
About whether God cared about someone like her, someone who'd done things she couldn't undo.

Joseph answered as best he could.

Sometimes he had words.
Sometimes he didn't.
Sometimes he just sat with her in the silence, letting the fire speak for them both.

“I'm sorry,” Joseph said quietly.

Soot looked up. “For what?”

“For not having better answers.”
He stared into the embers.
“You came to me with all these questions, and I just—”
He shrugged helplessly.
“I don't know how to explain it right. I don't have the words most of the time.”
“I just have… what I know. What I've felt.”

Soot was quiet for a moment.

“That's more than anyone's ever given me,” she said.

Joseph shook his head slowly.

“It doesn't feel like enough.”
He pulled his coat tighter around himself.
“Nothing I do feels like enough. I can't walk right. Can't carry things.”
“Can't help with camp or patrols or anything that matters.”
His voice dropped.
“I just take. Food. Shelter. Clothes. Their time. Their patience.”

Soot's brow furrowed.

“What are you saying?”

Joseph met her eyes, and there it was—raw and unguarded.

“I think I'm a burden,” he admitted quietly.
“To everyone who's helped me. To Eli and the others. I can't do anything but take.”

Soot stared at him.

“You?”
Her voice was almost incredulous.
“You feel like a burden?”

Joseph huffed a quiet laugh.

“All the time.”

Soot shook her head slowly.

“That's—”
She stopped. Started again.
“You're the only person who's ever made me feel like I wasn't one.”

The words landed softly between them.

Joseph didn't know what to say to that.
So he said nothing.
And somehow, that was enough.

He went on and told her about grace.

Of being loved before you're good.
Being wanted before you're worthy.
Being held not because of what you've done, but because of Whose you are.

Soot listened with her whole body—still, intent, like she was afraid of missing something.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment while she formed her next words.

“I still don't understand,” she whispered.
“How can someone just—”
She pressed a paw to her chest.
“How can He want this? After everything?”

Joseph looked at her steadily.

“Because He knows the real you,” he said gently.
“Not just what you've done. Not just what they call you.”
“The you underneath all of it.”

Soot's eyes were wet.
She didn't wipe them.

“That's stupid,” she said, but her voice cracked.

Joseph didn't respond.

He only pulled his coat tighter and watched the fire.
It had burned to embers.

Joseph didn't know how long they'd been talking.
His voice was hoarse, but something in his chest felt lighter than it had in weeks.

Soot's head rested on her paws, eyes on the ceiling.

She looked smaller now, somehow.
The sharp edges that had defined her earlier were gone, leaving someone younger, more uncertain, more visible behind.

For a moment, Joseph thought she might be beautiful.

He looked away quickly.

Soot stirred, as if sensing the shift.
Her gaze drifted to the shuttered window.

Grey light seeped through the cracks.

Soot's eyes widened.

“It's dawn.”

Joseph spun to see.

“Oh, golly,” his voice strained.

“You need to go. Now.”

Joseph scrambled to his feet, heart lurching.

“How long—”

“Hours.”
Soot was already moving, crossing to the door, unbolting it.
“They'll notice you're gone if they haven't already.”

Joseph grabbed his coat, fumbling with the sleeves.
His legs trembled with exhaustion and sudden adrenaline.

At the door, he paused.

Soot stood there, silhouetted against the grey light, and for a moment she looked like someone caught between two worlds.

“Thank you,” she said quietly.

Joseph met her gaze.

Soot's expression flickered—uncertain, raw, nothing like the sharp fox from the road.

“For not leaving,” she continued.
“On the road. When I couldn't—”
She shook her head.
“You could've walked. Should've, probably. But you stayed.”
“And then you told me all of it, and I just—”

She stopped, jaw working.

“I didn't expect any of this,” she whispered.
“I thought I'd find some crazy zealot.”
“Someone who'd shout at me or try to sell me something.”
“Instead I found—”
She looked at him directly.
“Someone who just… sat with me.”

Joseph stared at her moment, then nodded.

“I'm praying for you.”

Her eyes went wide—just for a moment.
Then she nodded once, sharply.

“Go,” she said.
“Before you're caught.”

Joseph ran down the street as best as his little paws could carry.
