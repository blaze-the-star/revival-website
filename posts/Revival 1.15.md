Joseph’s eyes opened to tent canvas.
His snout was cold from the night air.

His first thoughts rose clear and sharp.

_I’m done._

It arrived whole, like it had been waiting on the other side of sleep.

The canvas above him held the faint gray of early morning. Wind dragged cold along the seams. Somewhere outside, a bird called once and went quiet again.

Joseph stared.

His chest already hurt.

Not the sharp hurt of being struck, or scraped, or hungry.

A slow, constant weight—like he’d gone to bed with a stone pressed against his heart and woken to find it had grown roots.

He lay still, blanket pulled to his chin.

And the ache was there again.

Not vague.  
Not theoretical.

Absence.

Joseph decided he did care for his family.

He hadn’t known deciding would do this.

He hadn’t realized love was a door you could open and never close again. That once you admitted it—once you let it be true—the missing would become physical.

He saw them like quick strikes behind his eyes.

His mother’s hands in warm water.  
His dad’s laugh half-covered by a cough.  
A sibling calling his name from another room like it was nothing special.

Normal.  
Mundane.

And now unreachable.

The weight bred contempt the longer it sat there.

For this world.  
For this body.  
For the way the day would ask things of him like nothing had been stolen.

Joseph breathed in through his nose—slow, careful—like if he breathed wrong, something inside him would split open.

He didn’t cry.

Not yet at least, but it felt it there—waiting.

The yearning stayed in his chest like a bruise the day kept pressing.

---

The day passed.

Morning chores. Midday tasks. Eli giving him small work that kept his paws busy. Chatter filling silence with talk. Fight passing by like weather. The camp moving with the steady indifference of survival.

Joseph did what he was told.

He cut kindling. He carried bundles. He ate when food was put in front of him.

And the ache stayed.

It sat in his ribs when he lifted wood.  
It sat behind his eyes when he looked at the sky too long.  
It sat in his stomach when he swallowed bread that tasted like nothing.

The evening sat under everything like a rock on a wound.

Flint Water.

A place to speak openly.

He'd promised Soot that he would spread the gospel in town today—when the light was soft and people were lingering, when voices carried but the street wasn’t at full boil.

Joseph had not wanted to.

Being seen felt like stepping into a trap.

But he’d relented anyway—because he believed he should be doing it. Because he’d told Soot about a God who didn’t hide, and it felt like hypocrisy to keep his faith tucked behind a tent flap.

So the whole day, under every task, under every breath, the evening crawled closer.

As the sun tilted and shadows stretched long, Joseph’s hands started to shake—not from cold, but from time finally reaching him.

He found Eli near the edge of camp, where the trees started to tighten.

Eli looked up before Joseph even spoke.

> “What.”

Joseph swallowed. His throat already felt scraped raw just from existing.

> “I’m… I’m going into Flint Water,” he said.
> “For a bit.”

Eli’s eyes held him for a beat too long.

> “Stay out of trouble.”

Joseph nodded once and huffed as he walked away.
He knew what that meant.
He knew that was exactly what he was going to do.

---

Flint Water in the evening wasn’t quiet so much as it was _settling_.

Stalls half-shuttered. Lanterns being lit. Footsteps slowing. People speaking in tired, practical tones. The river caught the last light and dulled it to metal.

Joseph stayed to narrower paths, keeping his eyes down, letting groups pass before he crossed.

Soot had chosen a place out of sight: a cut between buildings where walls leaned in, where the air smelled damp and old—river stone, stale grain, wet wood. A lane that ended in stacked crates near the bank, hidden enough to talk before stepping into open air.

Joseph leaned his shoulder against the rough wood and waited.

Before Soot arrived, a voice carried from around the corner—female, sharp with affection and exhaustion at the same time.

> “Joker, honey—don’t touch that. You’ll burn yourself.”

It wasn’t his mother.

It wasn’t even meant for him.

But his lungs forgot how to move anyway.

The shape of it—the name held like something loved—hit him so hard his vision blurred instantly, like his eyes had been waiting all day for permission.

His throat tightened. His jaw clenched.

_No. Not here. Not now._

A tear slid down his muzzle anyway.

Then another.

His chest hitched—too sharp, too fast. His body tried to swallow the sound and failed. A small, strangled whimper leaked out through his teeth like a dog.

Joseph slapped a paw over his mouth.

Too late.

His shoulders shook again, harder this time, and he fought it like he could physically shove grief back down his throat. He pressed his tongue hard against his palate until it ached. He forced air through his nose.

But his breath stuttered, breaking into uneven little pulls that wouldn’t smooth out no matter how hard he tried.

Another whine, thin and broken, that wouldn't hold.

He hated it.

He pressed his forehead to the crate, as if pressure could pin the tears in place.

The whimpering kept slipping out anyway, stifled and ugly—caught behind his paw, vibrating in his jaw. Every time he thought he’d caught it, another one came, smaller, more helpless.

His chest spasmed. His throat burned.

He tried to swallow and it scraped like swallowing something sharp.

The tears didn’t stop. They ran and ran, warm into his fur, gathering in his jawline, dripping onto his sleeve.

Joseph shook his head once—small, furious, like he could shake it out of himself.

It didn’t work.

A wet, strained gasp tore out, and his paw couldn’t fully smother it.

He squeezed his eyes shut so hard white sparks burst behind his lids.

He forced a deep breath and released it slow.
Again—in. Out slow.
Folding it up, packing it away.

The shaking quelled.

The tears didn’t stop right away.

They kept coming in quiet waves, and every so often a tiny whine still slipped loose like a twitch.

But Joseph didn’t let it turn into sound again.

He wiped his face with his sleeve, angry at the wetness, angry at the proof.

One more inhale.
One more exhale.

A beat where he didn’t move.
A beat where he didn’t think.

Then he straightened, slow.
He drew a breath.
Barely under control.

Footsteps scuffed in the lane.

Joseph went still, swallowing hard.

---

Soot slipped into the cut between buildings like smoke.

Black fur. Bright eyes. Posture already sharpened with purpose.

She took one look at him and her expression changed—just a fraction.

> “You look… rough.”

> “I’m fine,” Joseph said too fast, voice thick.

Soot’s gaze narrowed--head tilted.

> “Sure you are.”

Joseph stared past her toward the street, pretending the river steps were the only thing that mattered.

Soot stepped closer—present, stable.

> “So,” she said, like she was talking about coin or weather.
> “You ready?”

Joseph’s stomach turned.
He nodded once. Small.

Soot’s mouth twisted like she could taste his fear.

> “Don’t do that,” she said.

Joseph blinked.

> “Do what?”

> “That little rabbit nod,” she snapped.
> “You’re not goin’ out there to apologize for breathin’. You’re goin’ out there to say somethin’ true.”

Joseph’s ears dipped.

> “I don’t want to—”

Soot’s eyes flashed.
Then—briefly—something softer moved behind them.

Not pity.
Not patience.

Something like recognition.

She built a wall over it immediately.

> “Tough,” she said.
> “You said you would.”

Joseph sighed.

> “I know. I just…” He swallowed, throat tight.
> “I’m not good at talking to crowds.”

Soot tilted her head at him, irritation already sharpening her posture.

> “You’re good at talkin’ to me.”

Joseph’s jaw tightened.

> “Last I checked,” he said, tired, almost matter-of-fact,
> “you were a crowd of fur, not people.”

It wasn’t meant as an insult.
He was just… *done*.

Soot went still.
Her ears angled back a fraction.

Joseph’s expression shifted, just slightly.
That was sharper than he thought it’d be.

For half a second, something like regret flickered in his eyes.

Then Soot snapped back, voice clean and edged.

> “Watch it, fox.”

The flicker vanished.

> “Yeah, yeah,” Joseph said dismissive, looking past her toward the main strip.

Soot studied him a moment longer.

Her eyes narrowed—not offended so much as recalibrating.

> “That ain’t fear,” she said, quieter now.
> “That’s resentment.”

Joseph didn’t answer.
His ears dipped, but he didn’t look at her.

Soot exhaled through her nose, sharp.

> “Look,” she said, straightening, tone snapping back into familiar hardness.
> “You wanna be angry at the world? Fine. Do it after.”

She jerked her chin toward the street.

> “Right now you got a job.”

Joseph’s paws curled slightly at his sides.

Soot stepped closer—not gentle, but focused.

> “You think I ain’t scared of them?” she said, voice low and fast.
> “You think I like standin’ where every eye’s waitin’ for me to slip?”
> “But this ain’t about them likin’ you.”
> “It’s about whether you believe what you’re about to say.”

Her eyes locked onto his.

> “You do. Or you wouldn’t be hurtin’ like this.”

Joseph swallowed. His throat still felt raw.

Soot took a half step back, reclaiming distance like she’d revealed too much by accident.

> “Go,” she said, flat but steady.  
> “Say it clean. Don’t spit it.”

Joseph hesitated only a second longer.

Then he turned toward the street.

Soot slipped back into the shadows at the edge of the lane, arms folding loosely across her chest.

Her posture looked indifferent.
Her eyes were anything but.

> “Don’t make me regret backin’ you,” she muttered, just loud enough for him to hear.

Joseph took a breath, then stepped into the open.

---

The river steps sat at the edge of a small open space where the street widened just enough for people to pause without blocking everything.

Just open enough that voices carried.

Joseph stood where the flickering lantern light hit his face and immediately felt exposed.

Eyes turned.  
Just a few, but it was enough.

A badger on a bench. A raccoon tying a bundle. Two coyotes lingering near a post, talking with that easy confidence people had when the world hadn’t asked anything painful of them today.

Joseph’s mouth went dry.
He forced his paws still and opened his mouth.
Nothing came.
His heartbeat filled his ears until it drowned his thoughts.

Joseph looked around.  
He found Soot, distant, pretending not to pay attention.  
She turned her head to him, barely, and nodded.

_Go on,_ he could almost hear her say.

Joseph swallowed and forced sound out before fear could lock it.

> “There’s a God who—”

His voice cracked.
Heat crawled up his ears.
He tried again.

> “There’s a God who sees you,” he pushed out.
> “He isn’t bought. He isn’t traded. He isn’t like—like the shrines.”

A badger snorted.

> “Sure,” someone muttered.
> “And what’re you sellin’?”

> “I’m not selling anything,” Joseph said too quickly.
> “I’m just—He’s real, and He—”

A voice rang out from the side—loud, sharp, amused.

> “Oi! That the little fox what didn’t bow?”

Joseph’s stomach dropped.

One of the coyotes had stepped forward a fraction, tall and rangy with a grin that didn’t feel friendly.

His eyes flicked over Joseph like he was measuring how much fun he could get out of him.

> “Thought you’d be gone by now,” the coyote called.
> “Or dead.”

A few chuckles rose—uncertain, testing.

Joseph’s throat closed.
He took one step back without meaning to.
The open air suddenly felt like it had teeth.

And panic made him stupid.

He went straight to Soot.
He cut across the edge of the space, beelining to where she stood in shadow.

Soot’s eyes flashed—sharp, alarmed.
She didn’t answer him.
She moved.
Soot turned and walked away around the nearest building like she didn’t know him, like she’d never seen him in her life.

Joseph stopped short, caught mid-step.
His chest tightened.
For one awful moment, his mind supplied the worst answer with perfect ease.

_She left._

His ears rang. His paws went cold.

Then—faint, from the darkness of an alley beside him—a hiss.

> “Fox.”

Joseph jerked his head.

Soot was half-hidden in the alley mouth, eyes bright in the dark.

Joseph stepped into the alley, heart pounding.

> “Not _there_,” she snapped under her breath.  
> “Not to me. You wanna talk, you come _here_.”

Joseph nodded too quickly.

Soot’s eyes narrowed.

> “And don’t do that either.”

Joseph swallowed.

> “They’re eatin’ you alive,” she said, not unkindly. Just factual.

> “I know.”

> “You’re lettin’ ’em.”

Joseph’s jaw tightened.
For a second he just stood there, chest rising and falling too fast.
Then it came out—desperate and too honest.

> “Maybe you should do it.”

Soot blinked once.

Joseph pushed through before he lost the nerve.

> “You have the fire for it,” he said.
> “You don’t freeze up. You actually—” He gestured vaguely toward the street.
> “You want this.”

Soot stared at him.
Her voice, when it came, was level.
Almost too level.

> “You forget who I am?”

Joseph hesitated.

Soot’s ears angled back just slightly—not in shame, but in cold recognition.

> “They don’t hear a ‘fire,’ fox,” she said.
> “They hear ‘angle.’ They hear ‘trick.’”

Her mouth twitched without humor.

> “Soon as I open my mouth about God, it won’t matter what I say. It’ll be ‘What’s she sellin’ now?’”

Joseph's head dropped with sigh.
He looked toward the mouth of the alley where Ridge’s voice still drifted in and out of the evening air.

> “So it’s me,” he muttered.

Soot followed his gaze.

> “You hear what he called you?”

Joseph’s shoulders tightened.

> “‘The one who didn’t bow,’” she said.

A beat.

> “Lean into it.”

Joseph frowned.
> “What?”

Soot turned fully toward him now.

> “You ain’t just some fox babblin’ about a new god. You’re _that_ fox.” Her voice went razor-clean.
> “The one who stood when priests told you to fold.”

Joseph swallowed. His throat still hurt.

Soot’s eyes locked onto his like she was pinning him in place.

> “Make ‘em deal with that first.”

A beat.

> “Go back out. And don’t you dare shrink.”

Joseph hesitated—just long enough to feel the terror.

Then he nodded once and stepped out of the alley.

Soot slipped back into motion too—peeling away from the alley mouth, melting to a different shadow at the outskirts, watching from distance.

---

Joseph walked back into the open.
His paws wanted to turn him around.
His chest wanted to collapse.
But Soot’s words stuck in his ribs like a hook.

_Lean into it._

Ridge’s grin widened when Joseph returned.

> “There he is,” Ridge called.  
> “Little shrine-defier.”

Joseph’s heart slammed.
He lifted his chin anyway.
His voice came out louder than it had any right to—thin, shaking at the edges, but carrying.

> “I _am_ the one who didn’t bow.”

The chuckles faltered.

A few heads turned more fully now, attention sharpening.

Joseph’s hands trembled at his sides; he curled his claws into his sleeves and kept going.

> “I didn’t bow because what you’re bowing to can’t hold you,” he said, voice catching, then steadying.
> “It can’t see you. Doesn't love you.”

For a heartbeat—one clean, impossible heartbeat—he was doing it.

People listened.
Not kindly.
But still _listening_.

Joseph’s chest burned with something that wasn’t just panic.  
Something like momentum.

Then Ridge laughed again—short, sharp, dismissive—and the sound cracked the fragile balance.

> “Oh, listen to him,” Ridge called.
> “Like he’s somebody.”

A different voice chimed in, cutting.

> “Yeah—somebody who thinks he’s better than us.”

Another snort.

> “Think your god’s gonna save you from teeth and winter?”

The attention sharpened into pressure.
Joseph’s mind started to scatter.

He knew he had an answer, and he tried to give it, but words piled up wrong in his mouth.  
His throat tightened again.
He heard himself start to ramble.

He saw eyes glaze.
He saw a badger’s mouth twist like he’d already decided Joseph was an annoyance.

Joseph’s voice faltered.
He backed up—one step, then two—until he was standing at the alley mouth again, breath coming too fast.

He stepped inside.

The shadows swallowed him.

Soot was already there, like she’d been waiting for the moment he cracked.
She met him halfway in the alley, eyes narrowed.

Joseph’s breath shook.

> “I did—” he started.  
> “I tried—”

> “You did better,” Soot cut in, quick and sharp. Then, quieter, with something almost grudging under it:  
> “For a second.”

Joseph swallowed.
> “It didn’t last.”

Soot’s jaw clenched.

> “It never lasts the first time.”

Joseph stared at her, anger and shame tangling together.

> “I can’t keep doing this.”

Soot’s eyes flashed.

> “Yes you can.”

Then it started, rising in Joseph's thoughts--bubbling up like magma.

_Of course you’d say that. You’re not the one out there._
_You get to hide and I get to be torn apart._
_You don’t even have to pay for this. I do._

Joseph snapped at himself, vicious.

_No! Stop it! Don’t think like!_

But the thoughts still bit at him.

Soot watched his face like she could see the war happening behind his eyes.
Then her voice dropped—fast, controlled.

> “Fox. You get one sentence. That’s it. One clean sentence. You don’t debate. You don’t answer every dog in the street. You say the truth and you let it land.”

Joseph’s mouth went dry.

Soot leaned closer, and for half a second the hardness slipped and something real showed through.

> “I need you to do this,” she said, barely audible.

Then the wall snapped back up.

> “Go,” she hissed.  
> “Before you talk yourself into bein’ a coward.”

Joseph flinched at the word.

Then he turned and stepped out of the alley.

Soot slipped away too—peeling off into the outskirts again, watching from distance, careful to never be seen leaving with him.

---

Joseph went out again.

One sentence.
He tried to keep it simple.

> “There’s a God who came close,” he said, voice shaking.
> “Who didn’t stay far away.”

Ridge scoffed.

Someone else cut in.

> “So where’s He now?”

Joseph’s thoughts scattered. His mouth opened.

Too many answers.
Too many ways to say it wrong.

A laugh rose from the edge of the space—different from Ridge’s, but joining him.

Joseph’s face heated.  
His throat closed.

But he didn’t run back.

He stayed there—because if he ran, he’d prove them right.  
Because the alley felt like a leash now.  
Because he could feel Soot watching from somewhere wide, and he hated that he needed it.

His mind started to spiral right there in the lantern light.

_She’s making you do this._
_She’s not the one bleeding._
_No—stop it._
_You’re doing her job._
_She picked you because she’s safe and you’re disposable._

Joseph’s ears rang.

His chest ached—home-ache, grief-ache—and shame layered on top like wet cloth.

He tried to speak again and nothing came out.

He stood there with his mouth half open and no sound, because he was overwhelmed by eyes, by noise, by his own mind turning on him.

And then—like a spark—he remembered something Soot had said earlier, sharp as a whip:

_You’re goin’ out there to say somethin’ true._

_You said you would._

It hit him wrong now.
Not courage.
Pressure.
And then, he remembered—

Soot walking away from him in the open.  
Like she didn’t know him.  
The hiss from the alley.

*Not there.*

The rule.
The distance.
And again—

*They don't hear 'fire.' They hear 'angle.'*

Joseph’s chest tightened.

If they saw him with her—
If they decided he was hers—

*They'd hear trick.*

Joseph turned.
Not back toward the river steps.
Toward the shadows where she kept herself apart.
He didn’t even fully understand what he was doing until he was doing it.

Soot’s eyes widened.
Joseph lifted his voice—just enough to carry.

> “You know what?” he said, turning so the river steps could hear.  
> “You want to laugh? Fine.”

Soot’s head snapped toward him.
And in her face—
Panic.
Real panic.
Real fear.

Soot made a choice in a heartbeat.
Her voice cut through the air, loud and ugly on purpose.

> “Get away from me, you creep!”

The words hit Joseph like a thrown stone, and they hurt... or shouldn't they have?  
Joseph felt them land, but not like pain.  
He couldn't understand it, but it felt more... invigorating.
It felt freeing.

It felt like **power**.

Joseph blinked, startled.

Then he laughed.
A real laugh—short, sharp, unhinged at the edges.

> “What’s wrong?” he called, voice light, stepping closer.
> “Afraid of lil’ ol’ me?”

Soot’s eyes flashed—furious and frightened all at once.

> “Get away!” she shouted, louder now.
> “Get away from me!”

She ran.

Joseph chased.
Because for one terrible moment, he felt free.

Free of being pushed.  
Free of being watched the
> “right” way.  
Free of having to be good.

> “Soot!” he called, breathless, laughing again.
> “Come on—”

A hand caught his scruff.

Joseph’s whole body jerked.

A sharp, involuntary yip—shot out of his muzzle as his legs flew out from under him.

He slammed onto his back, breath punched out of him, spine jolting against hard ground.
A paw, heavy on his chest, pressed him into the dirt.

Ridge loomed over him.
No grin now.
His eyes were hard, focused—protective in the worst way.

> “You stay away from her,” Ridge growled, low enough that it wasn’t for the crowd anymore.
> “You hear me? You don’t chase her.”

Joseph’s paws scrabbled uselessly at the dirt, panic cold in his veins.

Behind Ridge, Soot had stopped.

She turned back, eyes blazing.

> “Ridge!” she shouted.
> “Let him go!”

Ridge didn’t look away from Joseph.

> “He was chasin’ you.”

> “I don’t care,” Soot snapped. Her voice cracked sharp, and for half a second the mask slipped—raw fear under fury.  
> “Let. Him. Go.”

Ridge’s jaw tightened.

He hesitated—because Soot’s voice hit something in him that forced him to pause.

His grip loosened just enough.

Joseph didn’t wait for more.

He twisted, shoved his body sideways, wriggled out from under Ridge’s weight like prey slipping a jaw.
He hit all fours and bolted down the streets of Flint Water.

Gone.

Soot stood rigid.

Ridge straightened, chest heaving like he’d just won a fight he didn’t fully understand.

Soot stared into the darkness Joseph had vanished into.

For a beat, she didn’t move.

Then panic—held back by anger—flooded in.

Her chest felt too small.
Her throat tightened.

_No._
_No, no, no!_
_Not again!_

Soot turned on Ridge like he was the only solid thing left to blame.

> “What did you _do_?” she snapped, voice shaking with fury she couldn’t keep clean.
> “What did you say to him?”

Ridge bristled immediately.

> “I told him to leave you alone.”

> “You slammed him into the ground!” Soot hissed.
> “He’s half your size, Ridge!”

> “He was chasin’ you,” Ridge shot back, defensive now, fear turning into anger.
> “What was I supposed to do—just watch?”

Soot’s eyes burned.

> “You don’t get to decide what I need,” she said, voice low and poisonous.
> “Not like that. Not with me.”

Ridge’s jaw tightened.

> “Then what do you want?” he snapped.

Soot’s mouth opened—

And nothing came out.

Because what she wanted was impossible to say in front of the river steps and the lantern light.

Her gaze flicked once toward the darkness where Joseph had gone.
Her stomach twisted.
Inside—where no one could see—the blame turned inward like a knife.

_I pushed him._
_I knew he was already bleeding._

And the fear underneath was worse than the crowd, worse than Ridge, worse than her reputation.

Because it wasn’t just Joseph she might’ve lost.

It was the only place she’d started to believe she could be seen and not destroyed.

Soot forced her face back into hardness, because softness would ruin her.

> “Get out of my sight,” she said, low and deadly.
> “Before I forget you ever mattered.”

Ridge’s posture stiffened—pride flaring.

Then he turned and walked away, making sure he looked like he’d chosen to leave.

Soot stood alone as lanterns flickered and the river kept moving.

And in the space where he’d stood, something in her burned again.
