Joseph’s eyes cracked open to brightness and noise—voices.
Muffled.
Unfamiliar.
Urgent.

> “Found the lad lyin’ asleep in Chloros Forest, we did.”

> “Chloros? Who sleeps in a place like that, now?”

Joseph blinked slowly into half-consciousness, his thoughts thick like syrup. 
The accents were strange—half-old, half-southern, nothing he recognized.

_Weird dream already,_ he thought.

Only then did he feel the ground beneath him—coarse, gritty, cold.  
Not his mattress.  
Not his room.

He cracked an eye open.  
Sunlight stabbed his face immediately.

He draped an arm across his eyes.

> “Look there—he wakes,” said a woman’s voice.

Shapes swam into view: tents, smoke from a cooking fire, tools stacked neatly, birdsong, the sharp tang of river air.  
A wooden bowl appeared in front of him.

> “Here, we brought ya some water.”

Joseph leaned toward the bowl, curious—then stilled.

A reflection stared back at him.  
Not his face.  
Not even close.

Large pointed ears.  
A narrow muzzle.  
Soft fur shifting with his breath.

He tilted his head slightly. The reflection did the same.

A tiny spark of delight stirred in his chest.

_Oh… I'm a furry._

He touched his cheek, watching the reflection mimic him perfectly. The others around him tensed—but he barely noticed; he was too busy testing how the ears moved when he raised his eyebrows.

Footsteps crunched behind him.

> “He’s well outta sorts, ain’t he?” a dry voice murmured.

Joseph finally looked up.  
An otter, a coyote, and a bobcat stood in a loose, uneasy half-circle—like they expected him to bolt or bite. Their wary stares didn’t register as threatening to him; they were simply part of the scenery, the way dreams always sprinkled in strangers with intense opinions.

The otter crouched beside him.

> “Why wast thou out there, anyhow?”

“Out… where?” Joseph asked, genuinely puzzled.

“The forest,” the boy said slowly, as if speaking to someone concussed. “Chloros Forest.”

Joseph hummed, noncommittal.  
He wasn’t interested in answering their questions yet—he wanted to see how the light caught the fur on his arms. When he lifted his paw (paw!), the sunlight glimmered through the fine hairs. That fascinated him more than anything they were saying.

The coyote leaned closer, her voice low.

> “Dost thou hurt anywhere? Any blow to the head?”

“No,” Joseph said absently. He wiggled his fingers—short, soft, claw-tipped. _Very nice detail._

The bobcat exhaled sharply.

> “I ain’t dealin’ with this. I’m fetchin’ Eli.”

He stalked off with stiff annoyance.  
Joseph barely watched him go; he’d discovered the way his tail shifted when he leaned, and that was more engaging than a grumpy cat-man any day.

The otter nudged him again—insistent now.

> “Hey! Stay awake! ’Tis the middle o’ the day!”

Joseph blinked at him. “Mm? I’m awake.”

He wasn’t, not really.  
Everything had that soft, weightless quality dreams always had. The concern in their eyes slid off him like water.

Then the sunlight vanished.

A shadow fell across him—broad, deliberate.

He looked up at the towering shape.

And the dream changed.

A tall red wolf stood over him, scarred and stern, his presence cutting through the haze like cold water.  
The villagers straightened instinctively. Even the coyote lowered her ears.

> “You there,” the wolf said, voice deep enough to anchor the air.  
> “Name.”

Joseph blinked up at him.  
The wolf’s eyes were steady—not dream-soft, not fuzzy in the edges—just sharp.

“Joseph,” he answered lightly.

The wolf repeated it, slower.

> “Joseph.”

He crouched to Joseph’s level, and the world seemed to hold still around him.

> “’Twas told thou wert found senseless in Chloros Forest.  
> What business had thee wanderin’ there alone?”

Joseph tilted his head in thought.  
He felt no pressure, no urgency. His attention had snagged on the wolf’s teeth—long, yellowed, a little chipped. Good detail. Very grounded.

“I don’t remember being in a forest,” Joseph said truthfully, tracing a claw along the blanket’s edge.  
“I went to bed at home.  
Then I woke up… here.”

He sat up a little more. The blanket slid from his chest.

He froze.

He wasn’t wearing anything underneath.

A small, polite alarm rang somewhere deep inside him, like a far-off dinner bell.  
But the moment he tugged the blanket back up, the mild sense of embarrassment dissolved into that same dream-soft curiosity.

The wolf hadn’t reacted.  
His attention was on Joseph’s face, studying every twitch.

He turned to the bobcat.

> “Fight. Fetch me a map.”

They unrolled it on the ground.  
Hand-drawn contours. Markings. Rivers. Forests.

The wolf gestured.

> “Point to aught thou knowest.”

Joseph leaned over the map, scanning it.  
Forest. More forest. A long twisting river. Unfamiliar town names written in careful ink.

Nothing recognized him back.

He traced a claw over a mountain symbol just because he liked how the ink bled at the edges.

The bobcat eyed him.

> “What, thou ne’er seen a map before?”

Joseph didn’t bother responding to the tone.  
He just said softly, “I don’t know any of this.”

The coyote stepped closer, voice dropping.

> “Not even the river? The southern road? Aught at all?”

Joseph shook his head.  
She searched his face as if trying to find deception in it.

Instead she found… wonder?  
Detached calm?  
She couldn’t place it, and that unsettled her more than if he’d panicked.

The wolf leaned closer.

> “Joseph… what art thou?”

The question dropped like a stone.

Joseph blinked.

He looked down at his paw. Flexed it. Turned it over, admiring the flex of the small tendons beneath fur.

He didn’t feel fear.  
He felt the small, fizzing curiosity of handling a new puppet in a dream.

He lifted his eyes.

“What kind of question is that?”

The wolf didn’t answer.  
He only waited.

Something in his stillness pressed harder than his words.

Joseph swallowed.

“Can I ask something?” he said, almost cheerful. “A random thing?”

The wolf raised a brow, slow and expectant.

Joseph’s ears dipped a little.

“Do you… do you know what a human is?”

Everything stopped.

The coyote’s breath stalled.  
The otter’s whiskers twitched once, then froze.  
The bobcat’s tail curled tight with instinct.

The wolf’s expression shifted—not surprise, not fear, but recognition laced with sorrow.

> “…Thou art human?” he asked quietly.

For the first time, Joseph hesitated.  
A small crack formed in the dream’s surface.

“Well… yes,” he said. “I think so.”

The wolf closed his eyes for a long moment.

When he opened them again, the weight in them was no dream.

> “…Bless thy soul.”

The words were soft.  
Too soft.  
Too real.

Something cold rolled down Joseph’s spine.

“…Why would you say that?” he murmured.

The wolf stood.

> “Ready an extra tent,” he instructed.  
> “And rations fit for one more.”  
> “Find the lad some clothes.”

His voice had changed. No longer interrogating—preparing.  
As if Joseph were a problem to be handled, not solved.

The bobcat forced a grin.

> “Oh, grand. A guest.”

Joseph barely heard him.

For the first time, the world didn’t feel like a toy.  
The edges sharpened.  
The dirt pressed against his legs, real and gritty.  
His heart began to beat a little too hard.

Something was wrong.  
Deeply wrong.

The otter stepped closer, trying to regain his earlier levity.

> “It’s thy lucky day, truly! Food, a place to sleep—free as rain!”

He leaned in, voice dropping with genuine confusion.

> “Also… what’s a human?”

Joseph stared at the ground.

The dream didn’t feel so soft anymore.

> “I… I’m not convinced this is a dream,” he whispered.

And the weight of the forest, the camp, the watching eyes—  
all of it finally settled on his shoulders.
