Joseph surfaced from sleep like it was dragged up through dark water—slow, groggy, barely conscious of the world he was returning to.  
He shifted beneath his covers, expecting the soft cocoon of blankets and the hum of familiar warmth.

Instead, cold bit straight through his skin.

The ground beneath his back was coarse, uneven, gritty with dirt.  
A chill threaded through the air, sharp as claws and utterly unforgiving.  
He inhaled on instinct, and the smells hit him all at once—raw earth, lake water so clean it almost stung, wood-smoke drifting from some unseen fire, and other wild scents he had no names for.  
Each one was startlingly overwhelming, the kind of jarring strength that made his mind reel at how sharp everything suddenly seemed.

Unfamiliar voices pressed around him—quick, startled, overlapping as they argued in urgent hisses and low growls.

“—shouldn’t be lyin’ here—”

“—Chloros Forest, of all places—”

“—anybody unconscious out here means trouble for someone—”

There was an edge beneath the concern. Not fear—responsibility.
Joseph heard it without recognizing why.
These people were talking like professionals dealing with a problem they couldn’t afford to mishandle.

Joseph’s eyelids fluttered open. Light stabbed into them. The sun felt too bright, too close. He winced and squeezed them shut again.

A sudden shift rippled through the figures around him.

> “He’s wakin’,” someone murmured.  

> “Easy now—don’t spook.” another added.

A small voice piped up beside him.

> “Here, drink—or at least wet your face.”

Wooden scraping followed.  
Joseph blinked until shapes stopped doubling.

A round wooden bowl of water stood inches from his nose.

He lifted his head slowly, the blanket shifting down as he peered into the bowl — he paused.
He expected to see his reflection, but it didn't look right.  
The water shimmered the image of a slim snout, large pointed ears, and fluff pointing out from his cheeks.  

A fox.

He stared a moment too long, waiting for the reflection to correct itself.

It didn't.

> “Huh,” his voice low, almost dead sounding.

He lifting a hand toward his reflection—

Except his hand wasn’t a hand. A paw, slim and furred, touched the water’s surface, sending ripples across the creature’s face.

He paused, then poked his ear. It twitched. Reflexively.

His tired brain glowed with sudden childlike delight.

> “Hehe… that’s kinda neat,” he whispered to himself.

Everything still felt hazy, the world soft at the edges—dreamlike.  
Maybe that’s all this was.

He brushed his ear once more, pleased when it moved.

Voices sounded around him—tight, uncomfortable.

> “Ain’t the reaction I expected,” one muttered, tone edged with concern.

> “Folk found knocked out in Chloros don’t wake smilin’,” another added. Not accusing—just wary.

> “Could be he hit his head,” the smallest voice whispered uncertain—more like repeating what he’d heard the grown-ups say.
> “Or got dosed. He ain’t actin’ right.” 

Joseph tore his gaze from the water and found them.

They surrounded him—other animals standing upright, wearing simple work-worn clothes, each watching him not with wonder but with a hunter’s sharp, assessing professionalism.

The little otter boy shifted on his paws, tension flickering—then his face lit up with pride, as if remembering his moment to shine.

> “Name’s Chatter! I found ya! Well, Snout did the sniffin’, sure, but I laid eyes on ya first. That’s gotta be worth something.”

A coyote—lean, dusk-colored, with watchful amber eyes—eased forward.  
Her gaze swept him with slow precision, taking him in, but she said nothing.

Beside her stood a bobcat—broad-shouldered, arms crossed, looking like he’d skipped sleep and preferred it that way. His tail flicked once with irritation.

> “Fight,” he grunted.
> “And before ya ask: we ain’t here to coddle ya. Rules o’ the trade say we haul in anything suspicious we come across. You top that list, friend.”

Joseph’s playful disbelief faltered.  
_Suspicious? Rules of the trade?_  
_Dreams don’t come with laws…_  
_Well… maybe nightmares do._

Fight leaned in slightly, the weight of a man who’d asked this question a hundred rough mornings before.

> “So. Mind tellin’ us why you were passed out in the middle of the forest? And without a stitch on ya?”

Joseph blinked, startled. His gaze darted down.
The blanket had slipped to his shoulders—revealing he wore absolutely nothing underneath.
He yanked it back up to his neck so fast it snapped.

> “Why am I not wearing clothes!?” he yelped, clutching the cover around himself.

Fight shrugged, unimpressed.

> “We were hopin’ you’d answer that. We found ya like this. Out cold. Which means either you’re the victim of somethin’—or the cause of it. And either way, it’s on us to drag you in.”

> “Fight,” the coyote said quietly—just a soft nudge of restraint, enough to reel him back from his edge.

Joseph tried to sit up, embarrassment prickling across him.  
There were too many eyes on him. He was too exposed.

A strange, instinctive pressure pushed at him—_stand up, get steady, look like you belong_—and layered beneath that, a very human desire not to look helpless, naked, or afraid.

> “I—I’m fine,” he muttered.

Whether he said it to convince them or himself, he wasn’t sure.  
Either way, he braced and tried to rise.

His legs trembled at once.  
The angles were wrong—center of gravity skewed, feet shaped nothing like feet, toes missing entirely where he expected them, leaving him unable to plant or balance.  
For a moment he hung there in an awkward half-stand—

—and then his weight tipped forward uncontrollably as his unfamiliar limbs buckled beneath him.





Joseph hit the dirt with a soft thump—more humiliating than painful.  
He stayed where he landed, face still in the dirt, blanket draped over him like a defeated flag.

No one rushed forward. They didn’t look shocked—just concerned in the practical, professional way of people who’d seen every kind of injury but not this kind of behavior.

Chatter’s whiskers drooped.

> “Maybe he _is_ hurt…”

Joseph let out a long, unmistakably exasperated sigh—into the dirt.  
Not because he was hurt.  
Because they _believed_ he was.

He hated that.

Snout took a cautious step closer, posture lowered, paws open.

> “Easy now. Don’t force yourself. Just tell us straight—what part of you’s hurtin’? Legs? Head? Chest?”

Joseph pushed one elbow under himself, lifted his muzzle just enough to speak, then let the rest of him remain slumped.

His voice came out flat, edged with frustration he barely bothered to hide.

> “_Everything_ feels wrong.”

Snout blinked, taken aback—not by the attitude, but by the honesty.

> Fight grunted. “That ain’t helpful.”

Joseph didn’t respond.  
He refused to defend himself.  
Refused to argue.  
Let them think whatever they wanted—because explaining himself never changed anything, not back home, not now.

Snout tried again, gentler.

> “…Wrong how?”

Joseph hesitated.  
He didn’t know where to start.  
How to explain toes that didn’t exist, a body that moved wrong, a world that wasn’t his.

And they wouldn’t believe him anyway.

But Snout waited without pressing.  
And Fight—arms crossed, eyes squinting as if trying to read him—held back the next accusation.  
Even Chatter looked more worried than curious.

They weren’t guessing anymore.  
They were trying to _understand_.

Joseph sighed again—this time quieter, resigned.

He turned his head enough to meet someone’s eyes.

> “…I’m not hurt,” he said slowly.  
> “I’m…” He scoffed, eyes squeezing shut.  
> “I don’t know how to explain it.”

The coyote leaned closer, tone gentler than before.

> “We’re tryin’ to understand. Just give us somethin’ to go on.”

For a heartbeat, Joseph considered staying silent—letting them be wrong, letting them draw their own conclusions. It would be easier. It always was.

But they were actually trying. Searching his face. Wanting a real answer.

So he breathed once, heavy.

> “I’m human.”

They stared at him.

Blankly.

As though he’d just said a word that didn’t exist.

Chatter blinked.

> “A… what now?”

Fight rose slowly to his full height, expression flattening into something halfway between dread and annoyance.

> “Right, Okay… he’s crazy.”

Snout snapped her gaze toward him, ears pinning back.

> “Fight. Mind yer tongue.”

> “What? I’m just sayin’ what we’re all thinkin’,” Fight grumbled, though he took a half-step back as if trying to remove himself from the absurdity.

Snout shot him a warning look that could’ve cut bark.

Fight lifted his paws in surrender.

> “Fine. Fine. Look—**Eli** needs to deal with this.”  
> “You two stay here. Don’t let him try standin’ again.”

With a final huff, the bobcat turned and strode into the trees, tail flicking in frazzled irritation.

Chatter and Snout remained beside Joseph—uncertain, cautious, and trying hard not to stare too obviously at the odd, silent little fox sprawled in the leaves.

Chatter and Snout lingered beside Joseph in uneasy silence—Chatter fidgeting, Snout watching him with that same sharp, assessing stillness.

Joseph kept his face against the dirt, refusing to move, blanket bunched around him like a shield. His sigh still hung in the air—thin, exasperated, thoroughly done with all of them.

Snout tried again.

> “Easy now… Ain’t no shame in admitin’ what’s hurt.”

Joseph didn’t lift his head.

> “Nothing’s hurt,” he muttered into the ground.  
> “Everything just feels… wrong.”

Snout’s ears angled forward—concerned but confused.

> “Wrong how?”

> “I don’t know how to explain it,” he said sharply.  
> “And you wouldn’t believe me anyway.”

Chatter’s eyes widened at that—half fear, half awe.

Before Snout could press further, the underbrush thrashed behind them.

Fight returned—with another figure at his side.

A tall golden-furred **lion**, mane braided back, wearing a patched coat and a traveler’s satchel slung across his shoulder.  
He moved with the weary competence of someone who never rushed but was never slow.

Eli.

He stepped into view—and stopped.

His eyes landed on Joseph, small and blanketed, curled in the dirt.

His expression **shifted.**  
Barely.  
A tighten at the eyes.  
A momentary inhale.  
A recognition he forced back down in less than a heartbeat.

Snout noticed. Her ears tipped warily.

Fight cleared his throat.

> “This the one makin’ trouble. Says he’s… well.”  
> He gave Joseph a dismissive wave.  
> “Crazy.”

Snout snapped,

> “Fight—mind your manners.”

Eli didn’t respond to either of them.  
His gaze stayed on Joseph—steady, evaluating.  
Almost… searching.

Joseph felt it. Even through the blanket, even with his face half in the dirt.  
Someone was seeing him—not the fox body, not the assumed injuries, but _him_.

And for the first time since waking, Joseph hesitated.

Eli stepped closer. Slowly. Carefully.

> “You’re the one callin’ yourself ‘human’?”

His tone wasn’t mocking.  
Or skeptical.  
Or angry.

It was controlled. Too controlled.

Joseph looked up, eyes tired, ears angled flat.

> “Yeah,” he said softly. “I am.”

Snout watched Eli’s face like a hawk, waiting for his reaction.

Eli gave none.

But his next words came quieter.

> “…Tell me. When’d you start feelin’ wrong?”

Joseph frowned—not expecting that question.

> “Since I woke up,” he said.  
> “Everything felt weird shaped. I tried to stand”

A long, weighted pause.

Eli exhaled—slow, controlled.  
He looked briefly away, as if steadying something inside him, then back at Joseph.

> “Alright,” he murmured.  
> “Let’s get you up. Nice and slow.”

Fight scoffed.

> “Eli, he can’t stand. Fox hit the dirt like a sack o’ grain.”

Eli didn’t even glance at him.

> “He ain’t hurt,” he said. “Not in the way you think.”

Joseph’s breath caught.  
He hadn’t said that out loud.

Eli held out a steady, open hand—not grabbing, not assuming, just offering.

> “Come on,” he said. “Try again.”

Joseph hesitated, staring at Eli’s hand like it was a lifeline and a trap at once.

Eli’s voice softened, barely above a whisper:

> “You ain’t alone in this. Not near as much as you think.”

Joseph blinked.

Snout’s ears twitched.  
Chatter stared between them, confused.

Fight frowned, muttering,

> “…What’s that mean?”

Eli didn’t answer him.

He just waited—hand extended, gaze fixed on Joseph with an understanding he shouldn't possibly have.

The kind only another human would know.

Eli’s grip was steady, warm, impossibly sure.  
Joseph braced himself, trying to rise on trembling legs that didn’t want to cooperate. His new paws scrabbled uselessly against the dirt, his center of gravity sliding every which way.

Eli adjusted instantly.

A broad arm braced behind Joseph’s back—not touching, just there, ready to catch him without forcing the contact. Joseph leaned into it anyway, unsteady.

His legs quivered, pads slipping sideways.

> “Easy,” Eli murmured. “Your body’s readin’ itself different than your mind remembers. Give it a breath.”

_Different._  
_Than he remembers._

The phrasing prickled at Joseph.  
How could Eli possibly understand that?

He tried again.  
Got halfway up before his hind-paws betrayed him, sliding out from under him like he was standing on marbles.

He toppled forward.

Eli caught him gently by the upper arms.

Not a flinch.  
Not a grunt.  
Like he’d expected the failure.

Behind them, Fight let out a low whistle.

> “See? Told y’all he can’t stand. Probably concussed.”  
> His gaze narrowed at Joseph, suspicion growing.  
> “Or fakin’ helpless.”

Eli’s head snapped toward him with a sharpness that shut the bobcat up mid-breath.

> “He’s not fakin’,” Eli said, voice level but edged.  
> “And he’s not hurt. He’s just… off-balance.”

Snout’s ears dropped back, thoughtful.

> “…Like his body ain’t familiar to him?” she asked quietly.

Joseph froze.

Eli didn’t answer.  
His silence was answer enough.

He still had a hand on Joseph’s back, steadying him.  
Joseph felt small beneath his touch—a child learning to walk.  
He hated the helplessness.  
Hated how easy it was for Fight to assume the worst.

And hated how right they were to think something was wrong.

Eli eased him back down to a seated position, slow and careful.

Then the wolf rose to his full height.

From Joseph’s angle, it felt like watching a mountain stand.

Eli dusted his palms off, then looked to Snout and Fight.

> “Get him clothes,” he said.  
> “Something basic—shirt, trousers, belt. And tell camp to provision for one more.”

Fight stiffened.

> “…One more? Eli, hold up—”

Snout blinked, taken aback.

> “You mean to _keep_ him with us?”

Eli’s gaze dropped back to Joseph.

Not unkind.  
Not pitying.  
Resolved.

> “He’s comin’ with us.”

The decision landed heavy in the clearing—like a stone dropped into still water.

Fight threw up both hands.

> “You’re claimin’ him? Off just his word and a tumble in the dirt? Eli, for all we know he _did_ somethin’—”

> “And for all we know he didn’t,” Eli cut in.  
> “We’ll sort it out when he can stand without fallin’ on his nose.”

Snout’s tail flicked, uncertain but not protesting.

Fight muttered something under his breath but trudged off toward camp, Snout at his side.

Their footsteps faded.

Joseph stared after them, blanket clutched tight around his neck.

Eli crouched beside him again, the titan folding down to meet the tiny fox.

His voice softened.

> “You’re safe with us. We’ll figure this out.”

Joseph’s throat worked.

He wasn’t used to people stepping in.  
He wasn’t used to being helped without being judged.  
He wasn’t used to anyone choosing to keep him close.

And now they were going to _give_ him clothes?  
Make room for him?  
Bring him along?

He didn’t deserve that.

A heavy, sour guilt crawled through his chest.

He lowered his gaze.

> “…You shouldn’t do that,” he murmured. “I don’t… I’m just going to end up using you. Without meaning to.”

Eli’s ears twitched, a flicker of something unreadable crossing his face.

> “You ain’t usin’ anyone,” he said quietly.  
> “You’re lost. That’s all.”

Joseph swallowed hard, unable to meet those steady amber eyes.

Eli stood again, towering, silhouette framed by the early light.

> “Rest a minute. I’ll make sure they bring the right fit.”

He turned to go.

Joseph watched him—this impossible giant who understood things he shouldn’t—and felt the chapter of his life closing behind him, another opening before him.

One he never asked for.  
One he wasn’t sure he deserved.

But for now…  
He was not alone.
